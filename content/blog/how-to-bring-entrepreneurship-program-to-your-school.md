If you're a Michigan high school administrator or teacher who has decided your students would benefit from a real entrepreneurship program, the next question is logistics: how do you actually get one running?

This is a short, practical playbook based on what we've seen work — and what we've watched stall — across our partner schools in Oakland County and the broader Detroit metro.

## Step 1: Pick the slot before you pick the program

The single biggest predictor of whether an entrepreneurship program survives its first year is whether it has a dedicated time slot. Programs that try to live "in addition to" everything else die quickly. Programs that fit into an existing class period thrive.

Common slots that work well in Michigan high schools:

- **CTE business courses** — perfect fit; SparkLab's curriculum maps directly.
- **Economics or personal finance** — same audience, complementary content.
- **A senior elective** — often the easiest political path with the most flexible scheduling.
- **An "innovation block"** if your school runs one.

Pick the slot first. Then evaluate which program fits the slot.

## Step 2: Get one teacher actively excited

A district mandate without a teacher champion goes nowhere. A teacher champion without district support goes further than you'd expect.

The right teacher does not need to have entrepreneurial experience. They need to be:

- Comfortable being a *facilitator*, not a sage.
- Willing to bring in outside mentors as the primary "experts".
- Excited about students building messy, real, imperfect things in front of them.

If you have that person, you have a program.

## Step 3: Decide what "real" means

Some "entrepreneurship" programs are entirely simulated. Students write business plans for a fictional business and present to their teacher. Others — like SparkLab — push students to identify real problems, validate them with real people, and present to real working entrepreneurs.

The simulated path is easier to run. The real path produces dramatically better outcomes — and dramatically better portfolio pieces for college applications.

Pick on purpose.

## Step 4: Lock in a culminating event

Programs that end with a real pitch event get taken seriously by students from week 1. Programs that end with a paper submitted to the teacher get taken seriously by no one.

The pitch event doesn't have to be elaborate. It can be:

- A panel of 3-5 local entrepreneurs evaluating teams in the school auditorium.
- A regional pitch competition with multiple schools competing for cash prizes (this is what SparkLab does).
- An evening showcase open to parents, community members, and local press.

What matters: it has to feel real, it has to be on the calendar from day 1, and there have to be consequences (good ones — recognition, prizes, exposure).

## Step 5: Talk to a few schools who already do it

Don't reinvent the playbook. Reach out to nearby Michigan districts that already run an entrepreneurship program and ask what they wish they'd known.

## A simple timeline

If you start the conversation today, here's a realistic timeline:

- **Now:** Identify the slot and teacher champion.
- **+30 days:** Evaluate 2-3 program options. Brief your principal and (if needed) district CTE coordinator.
- **+60 days:** Lock in the partner, dates, and venue for the pitch event.
- **+90 days:** First cohort starts.
- **+5 months:** First pitch competition.

That's one full school semester from "we should do this" to students standing on stage pitching real businesses.
