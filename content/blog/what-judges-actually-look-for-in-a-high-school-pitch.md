If you're a student about to pitch in a high school business competition — SparkLab's or anyone else's — there are a few things judges are quietly looking for that nobody tells you about. Here they are.

## We can tell within 30 seconds

This isn't to scare you. It's to focus you.

Within the first 30 seconds of a pitch, most judges have formed a working impression of whether the team in front of them has actually *done the work* — or whether they're winging it on top of a polished slide deck.

The signal is rarely about polish. It's about whether you can clearly articulate:

1. **Who has the problem you're solving.**
2. **How you know they have it.**

If you can answer both crisply in the first 30 seconds, the rest of the pitch tends to land. If you can't, judges spend the rest of your time looking for evidence you're making it up.

So: open with the customer. Always.

## The four things we score on

At SparkLab, every team is scored on four equally-weighted dimensions:

### 1. Business viability

Does this opportunity actually exist? Is the addressable market real? Do the unit economics make sense at any scale? Could this generate revenue?

This is not asking whether you've already made money. It's asking whether your model *could* work.

### 2. Innovation & creativity

What makes this idea original? Why now? What's your unfair advantage as a team — your access to a specific customer, a specific insight, a specific skill?

Note: "Nobody else is doing this" is almost never true. If nobody is doing it, the better question is *why not*.

### 3. Market understanding

This is where most high school pitches lose points. Judges want to hear that you've actually talked to potential customers. Not surveyed your classmates — talked to real people in the actual market.

If you can say "we interviewed 12 people in our target market and learned [specific thing]", you're already ahead of 80% of teams.

### 4. Presentation quality

This is the one most teams over-index on. It matters, but it's the lowest-ranked dimension because it's the easiest to fake. A team with a beautiful deck and no customer insight will lose to a team with a rough deck and real customer insight.

The presentation things that actually matter:

- Can you handle a hard question without flinching?
- Does every team member speak?
- Do you finish on time?
- Do you sound like a team that has actually worked together for 8 weeks?

## What separates winning pitches from losing pitches

After scoring a lot of high school pitches, the patterns are pretty consistent:

**Winners** open with the customer, show one specific insight from real research, defend their numbers with quiet confidence, and end with a clear ask.

**Runners-up** have great delivery but their model has a hole one of the judges catches.

**Everyone else** opens with the team, spends too long on the product, treats market size as "the global TAM is $40B", and gets thrown by the first hard question.

Aim for the first category. The good news is that aiming for it doesn't actually require natural talent. It requires doing the work in weeks 1-7 so you have something real to show in week 8.

## Practical tips for the week of the pitch

- **Run your pitch in front of someone you respect** who has not seen it before. Their reaction is the truest signal you'll get.
- **Time it.** If you're given 5 minutes, your pitch should land at 4:30, not 5:01.
- **Decide who answers which question type** in advance. Don't make the judges watch you whisper-coordinate on stage.
- **Bring a backup.** A USB stick, a PDF on someone's phone — assume the projector will fail and you'll need to wing it.
- **Eat before the pitch.** This sounds dumb. Half the disasters we've watched were caused by a student going on stage on empty.

That's most of what we can tell you without showing our rubric. The rest you learn by getting up and pitching.
